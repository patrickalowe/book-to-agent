# The Coaching Habit
**Author:** Michael Bungay Stanier · **Summarized:** 2026-08-20

## Overview
*The Coaching Habit: Say Less, Ask More & Change the Way You Lead Forever* (2016) argues that coaching shouldn't be a special event reserved for formal sessions — it should be a daily management habit that takes ten minutes or less. Michael Bungay Stanier's central claim is that most managers default to advice-giving: they leap in with solutions, create dependency, and rob their people of the chance to think. The antidote is to stay curious a little longer and rush to action and advice a little more slowly. The whole method reduces to seven deceptively simple questions.

Bungay Stanier is the founder of Box of Crayons, a training company that has taught coaching skills to tens of thousands of managers at organizations worldwide. He wrote the book to make coaching practical and un-intimidating for busy people who don't see themselves as "coaches." His framing draws on behavior-change science — using the cue/routine/reward habit loop — to help the seven questions actually stick, rather than being one more technique that fades after a workshop.

The book matters because it reframes coaching as a lightweight, repeatable behavior rather than a time-consuming discipline. Its promise is that by asking better questions and resisting the urge to fix, managers build more self-sufficient teams, do less low-value work themselves, and have more real impact.

## Core Values
- **Curiosity over advice.** The instinct to jump in with the answer is the enemy. Staying curious longer surfaces the real issue and respects the other person's ability to think.
- **Say less, ask more.** Genuine questions unlock ownership and insight in a way that telling never can. The manager's job is to draw out, not to dispense.
- **Building self-sufficiency, not dependency.** Good management develops people who can solve their own problems, rather than a team that funnels every decision back to the boss.
- **Focus on what truly matters.** Time and attention are scarce; coaching should steer people toward the challenge that actually deserves their energy, not just the first thing they mention.
- **Habits over intentions.** Lasting change comes from small, well-designed behaviors triggered by real-world cues — not from good intentions or one-off training.

## Key Principles
- **The Kickstart Question — "What's on your mind?"** Open with it to get quickly to what matters to the other person, cutting past small talk into a real conversation.
- **The AWE Question — "And what else?"** The single most useful coaching question; asking it a few times surfaces more (and usually better) options and stops you from acting on the first answer.
- **The Focus Question — "What's the real challenge here for you?"** Move past the first problem stated to the one that actually matters, and make it personal to *them* — not an abstract issue.
- **The Foundation Question — "What do you want?"** Name the underlying want or need directly, since so many conversations stall because it's never made explicit.
- **The Lazy Question — "How can I help?"** Force the other person to make a direct request, which stops you from assuming, over-functioning, and jumping in to rescue.
- **The Strategic Question — "If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"** Treat every commitment as a trade-off, so people focus and protect against overload.
- **The Learning Question — "What was most useful for you?"** Close every interaction with it, because people learn not from experience itself but from reflecting on it — and it reinforces the value of the conversation.
- **Tame your Advice Monster.** Notice and resist the reflex to offer solutions; the moment you have the answer is exactly when to ask one more question instead.
- **Build the habit deliberately.** Use the cue–routine–reward loop: identify the trigger, keep the new behavior tiny, and practice one question until it's automatic before adding another.

## Key Takeaways
- Coaching is a daily 10-minutes-or-less habit, not a formal event — the goal is to make it your default management reflex.
- "And what else?" and "What's the real challenge here for you?" are the two highest-leverage questions; master those first.
- Advice-giving feels helpful but creates dependency and often solves the wrong problem; stay curious a little longer.
- Ask genuine questions, then be quiet — the value is in the other person's thinking, not your answer.
- Habits change behavior; design a specific cue and start impossibly small so the new question actually sticks.
